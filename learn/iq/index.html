1. Orientation: what IQ tests measure

An IQ score compares your performance with a norm group of people your age. Most tests scale it to a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15, so 130 is about the 98th percentile (Mensa's usual cut). Different tests weight different abilities, which is why one person can score 10 points apart on two of them.

g, fluid and crystallized ability

g (general ability) is the statistical fact that people who do well on one kind of mental test tend to do well on all the others (Spearman, 1904). In a broad battery it usually explains the largest single share of score differences. Modern models (Cattell-Horn-Carroll, CHC) put g at the top and a set of broad abilities underneath: fluid reasoning (Gf), comprehension-knowledge (Gc), visual processing (Gv), working memory, processing speed and others.

Fluid intelligence is reasoning with new material: matrices, series, figure weights, most spatial items. It peaks in early adulthood and drifts down with age. Crystallized intelligence is knowledge and skill you have built: vocabulary, information, verbal analogies. It keeps rising into late middle age (Cattell, 1963; Horn & Cattell, 1967). For you this matters: verbal sections play to a lifelong strength; fluid and spatial sections are where unfamiliarity costs the most and where prep pays best.

How much does practice really help?

  • Retesting raises scores, modestly. A meta-analysis of 107 samples (134,436 people) found an average gain of about a quarter of a standard deviation (d ≈ 0.26, roughly 4 IQ points) from the first to the second sitting. Gains were larger with coaching (about 0.70 vs 0.24 without) and with the identical form (Hausknecht et al., 2007). A later meta-analysis of 122 studies found about a third of an SD on the first retest and no further gains after the third sitting (Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018).
  • Those gains are mostly not gains in g. Score increases from practice and training load on test-specific skills, not on the general factor (te Nijenhuis, van Vianen & van der Flier, 2007). You become better at the test; you do not become smarter.
  • Spatial skill is genuinely trainable. Across 217 training studies, spatial training produced an average effect of about half an SD (g = 0.47), lasted over time, and transferred to untrained spatial tasks (Uttal et al., 2013). That is the best evidence base of any family here, and the reason this course leans on it.
  • Brain-training working memory does not transfer far. Working-memory training improves the trained task and close cousins, but not reasoning in general (Melby-Lervåg & Hulme, 2013). The WM module therefore teaches test strategy, not "brain training".

What this means for you. Expect prep to remove avoidable losses: unfamiliar formats, slow starts, bad pacing, careless traps. That shows up as points, but averages hide wide individual differences and no one can promise a number. If a test is for a clinical decision (for example an ADHD or learning evaluation), light familiarity is fine, but heavy drilling on look-alike subtests can blur what the clinician needs to see. Tell the examiner if you have prepared.

2. The question families

Verbal: analogies, vocabulary, similarities, odd-one-out, reasoning

What it tests. Stored word knowledge and verbal abstraction (crystallized ability). Wechsler Similarities and Vocabulary, SB5 Knowledge, the CogAT Verbal battery and many Wonderlic items.

How items look. Analogies (A : B :: C : ?), vocabulary (define, or pick the synonym), similarities ("How are X and Y alike?", answered aloud on the WAIS), odd-one-out, and short passages with a must-be-true question.

Method

  1. Name the relation as a sentence before reading the options. "A fetter restrains movement."
  2. Plug each option into the sentence. If two fit, sharpen it: add degree, direction, part/whole, function, or lack ("a prodigal person lacks thrift").
  3. Check form. Same part of speech, same direction (whole → part on both sides).
  4. Similarities: say the most general category that is true of both and central to each. A concrete shared feature earns partial credit at best; a difference earns none.
  5. Vocabulary: give a precise definition plus a use. For multiple choice, use roots and connotation, and distrust sound-alikes.
  6. Must-be-true passages: pick the option that cannot be false, not the one that is most likely.

Common traps

Association instead of relation (DOCTOR : HOSPITAL is location, not tool). Reversed direction. Picking the definition when the stem asks for the lack (craven ≠ cowardice). Sound-alike distractors. In similarities, answering with a difference or an incidental feature. For strong writers, the main risk is over-reading: test writers key the standard sense, not the clever second meaning.

Worked examples

Answer: judge. Sentence: "A scalpel is the tool a surgeon works with." A gavel is the tool a judge works with. Courtroom is a place, hammer is a kind of tool, verdict is an output.

Numerical: series, word problems, quantitative reasoning

What it tests. Quantitative reasoning: spotting numeric rules and translating words into arithmetic. WAIS Arithmetic (mental, timed), SB5 Quantitative Reasoning, CogAT Quantitative, most of the Wonderlic's harder items.

How items look. Number series (find the next term), short word problems (rates, percents, work, mixtures), and quantitative comparisons (which is larger, or can't be told).

Method

Number series, in this order:

  1. First differences. Then second differences.
  2. Ratios (×2, ×3, ÷2).
  3. Split odd and even positions (two interleaved series). Big up-and-down swings are the tell.
  4. Known sequences: squares, cubes, primes, triangular numbers, Fibonacci-type (each term = sum of previous two), factorials.
  5. Alternating operations (×4, −2, ×4, −2).
  6. Mixed: ×2 + 1, n² − 1.

Word problems: name the unknown, translate one clause at a time, estimate the answer's size first, check units, and plug the answer back into the original words.

Facts worth having cold: rates add, times don't (work problems). Successive percents multiply. Average speed over equal distances is the harmonic mean, below the simple average. n gaps need n + 1 posts.

Common traps

The fast answer (bat-and-ball type: the "more than" difference). Averaging speeds. Adding percent changes. Off-by-one counting. Misreading "how many more" as "how many". On the WAIS, Arithmetic is spoken and timed with no paper, so translation speed matters more than computation.

Logical: syllogisms, ordering and seating, if-then

What it tests. Deductive reasoning: what must follow from stated facts. Appears in Wonderlic, Mensa-style tests, the new WAIS-5 Set Relations subtest, and most aptitude batteries.

How items look. Syllogisms (All/Some/No statements, does the conclusion follow?), ordering and seating puzzles, and if-then rules.

Method

Syllogisms. Try to build a counterexample: a world where both premises are true and the conclusion false. If you can, it doesn't follow. Quick rules: two negative premises give no conclusion; the middle term must be covered by "all" or "no" at least once; "some A are B" doesn't give "some A are not B". Draw three overlapping circles if unsure.

Ordering and seating. Draw the slots. Place fixed facts first, then pairs and blocks ("X directly right of Y"), then negatives. If stuck, branch on two cases and kill one. At the end, re-read every clue against your answer. For round tables, fix one person to remove rotations, and only use "left/right" if the puzzle defines it.

If-then. From P → Q you may conclude: not Q → not P. You may not conclude Q → P or not P → not Q. "A only if B" means A → B. "A unless B" means not B → A.

Common traps

Belief bias: judging by whether the conclusion sounds true. Reading "some" as "some but not all". Affirming the consequent. In Wason-type card tasks, checking the confirming card instead of the one that could falsify.

Worked example

Worked exampleSyllogism
All marketers are optimists. Some optimists are investors. So some marketers are investors?
Does not follow. Counterexample: the optimists who invest are all engineers. Both premises stay true; the conclusion fails.

Fluid / abstract: matrices, series, figure weights, odd figure

What it tests. Reasoning with new, nonverbal material: the purest fluid tasks and the closest thing to g on most batteries. WAIS Matrix Reasoning and Figure Weights, Raven's, Mensa tests, CogAT figure matrices.

How items look. 3×3 matrices with a missing cell; a row of figures with the next one missing; balance scales with shapes; sets of figures with one that doesn't belong.

Method

  1. List the attributes that vary: shape, number, size, shade, position, orientation, line overlays.
  2. Find a rule for each attribute separately, checking rows first, then columns, then diagonals. The common rules: constant; progression (+1, rotate 45°); distribution of three (each value once per row and column); addition, subtraction or exclusive-or of overlaid lines.
  3. Predict the missing cell before looking at the options. Then match.
  4. Eliminate any option that breaks any rule. If two survive, you missed a rule; if none, one rule is wrong.
  5. Figure weights: convert everything into the lightest shape, one scale at a time.
  6. Odd figure out: count before you look: sides, dots, lines, intersections, closed vs open, symmetry axes.

Common traps

Fixating on one striking attribute and missing a second rule. Choosing the option that "looks right" without checking columns. On union vs exclusive-or overlays, test the rule on two complete rows first. Last-resort guess: distractors are usually built by changing one attribute of the answer, so the option sharing the most features with the others is often right.

3. Spatial reasoning (the core of this course)

Spatial ability (Gv in the CHC model) is the skill of building, holding and transforming mental images. On the WAIS-5 it has its own index (Block Design, Visual Puzzles); on the SB5 it is one of five factors; on the CogAT it is half the nonverbal battery (paper folding). It is also the family where training has the strongest evidence: about half an SD on average, durable and transferring to untrained spatial tasks (Uttal et al., 2013).

Spatial items feel like they need a "visual" mind. In practice the best performers use procedures, and many of them are verbal. You are a strong verbal thinker; use it.

Core techniques

1. Anchor features

Don't rotate the whole shape. Pick one distinctive feature (the odd arm, the dot, the notch) and a second one. Find both in the option, then check that the second sits where it should relative to the first. Two anchors fix a 2D shape; three fix a 3D one.

2. Chirality check (rotated vs mirrored)

A rotation keeps the clockwise order of features; a mirror reverses it. Walk clockwise around the target naming features ("long arm, notch, short arm"), then walk clockwise around the option. Same order: rotation. Reversed: mirror. For 3D, use a hand rule: point along the first arm with your index finger, bend your middle finger along the second arm, and ask whether the third arm goes the way your thumb points. A mirror image needs the other hand.

3. Verbalise rotations

Encode the object in words: "three out, two left, two up." Words survive in memory longer than images, and comparing two descriptions is faster than comparing two pictures. Mental rotation time grows roughly linearly with the angle (Shepard & Metzler, 1971), so always rotate the shorter way, and for 180° think "flip top-to-bottom and left-to-right" instead of turning.

4. Opposite-face rules for nets

In a straight run of three squares, the first and third are opposite. In a zig-zag, squares one step apart along the same row or column are opposite. Opposite faces can never be seen together on a cube, and every face touches exactly four others. To check a pictured cube, take one corner: the three faces meeting there must appear in the same clockwise order as they do around that corner on the net.

5. Layer and column counting

Count blocks by columns (height read off the top face) or by layers (bottom layer first, then each layer up). Write the numbers on a mental plan view. For hidden blocks, apply the support rule: any visible block above floor level has a block under it.

6. Reverse the operation

Paper folding: unfold in reverse, mirroring every hole across each crease. Cross-sections: count how many faces the plane crosses; that is the number of sides. Embedded figures: hunt for the target's rarest feature first.

4. Test-day strategy

Pacing

  • Compute your per-item budget before you start: Wonderlic is 50 items in 12 minutes (about 14 seconds each). Mensa Norway is 35 in 25 minutes (about 43 seconds), but difficulty rises, so bank time on early items.
  • Set checkpoints at a quarter, half and three-quarters of the time. If you're behind, speed up on the easy-looking items, not the hard ones.
  • One-to-one tests (WAIS, SB5): the examiner controls pace and items come one at a time. Some subtests are strictly timed (Block Design, Visual Puzzles, Figure Weights, Coding, Symbol Search); others, like Matrix Reasoning, have no strict limit. Fast correct Block Design builds can earn time bonuses.

Guessing

  • Most IQ tests count only correct answers. Mensa Norway says so explicitly. So never leave a blank on a group test; read the instructions to confirm.
  • Smart guesses: eliminate options that break any rule you've found. In matrices, prefer the option sharing the most features with the other options.

When to skip

  • On group tests, if you've used twice your per-item budget and still have no rule, mark your best guess, flag it, and move on. Return only if time remains.
  • On one-to-one tests you can't skip. Give your best answer; the examiner stops each subtest after a run of misses, so a reasoned guess never hurts.

Sleep, caffeine and the body

  • Sleep: a normal night matters more than last-minute practice. Even short-term sleep loss clearly impairs attention and working memory (Lim & Dinges, 2010). Don't cram the night before; do 10 easy items as a warm-up instead.
  • Caffeine: your usual amount at your usual time. In regular users, much of caffeine's apparent benefit is reversing withdrawal (James & Rogers, 2005), so skipping it hurts and doubling it adds jitters, not points.
  • Nerves: a racing heart is readiness, not a warning. Telling yourself that arousal helps performance improved GRE math scores in one well-known study (Jamieson et al., 2010).
  • Eat beforehand, bring glasses, arrive early, and use the bathroom before the start. Long one-to-one sessions can run 90 minutes.

Mid-test resets

  • After a hard item, take one slow breath before the next. Items are scored independently; a miss is only one point.
  • If a spatial item won't resolve, switch method: from imagining to rules (chirality walk, opposite faces, layer counts).

9. Cheat sheet

One page. Print from the standalone cheat sheet file, or read it here.

Spatial (your main lever)

  • Rotated vs mirrored: pick two anchor features; walk clockwise naming them. Same order = rotation, reversed = mirror.
  • 3D cubes: encode arms in words (“3, turn, 2, turn, 2”); same sequence first, then the hand rule on the last turn. Rotate the short way.
  • Paper folding: unfold last fold first; mirror every hole across each crease; one layer under the punch = one hole.
  • Cube nets: run of three = ends opposite; zig-zag = skip-one opposite. Opposites never show together. Check corner order and which edge an arrow points to.
  • Block counting: column heights from top faces, then add. Every raised block needs support. Painted n³: 3 faces 8 · 2 faces 12(n−2) · 1 face 6(n−2)² · 0 faces (n−2)³.
  • Views: stand at the arrow, name your left and right. Front = tallest stack in each line of sight. Top = footprint. Opposite side = left-right flip.
  • Cross-sections: sides = faces the plane crosses (cube: 3–6). Parallel faces give parallel sides. Cylinder: circle, ellipse, rectangle. The drawing is in perspective; answer from the solid.
  • Embedded figures: hunt the rarest feature first; same size and orientation; one missing edge = reject.
  • Block design: grid the design first; corner, edge, middle; turn the block to find the face. Visual puzzles: area check, then the most awkward piece; no flipping.
  • Maps: heading as quarter-turns (R +1, L −1, about +2). Rotated map: page bearing minus the north arrow's angle.
  • Patterns: test mirror symmetry and repeating tiles; predict the patch, check all four neighbours.
  • Stuck? Switch from imagining to a rule: chirality walk, opposite faces, layer count.

Fluid / matrices

  • List attributes: shape, number, size, shade, position, orientation, lines.
  • One rule per attribute; rows, then columns, then diagonals.
  • Rules: constant, progression, each-value-once, add/subtract/XOR overlays, rotation.
  • Predict the cell before reading options. Two survivors = a missed rule.
  • Figure weights: convert to the lightest shape, one scale at a time.
  • Odd figure: count sides, dots, lines, symmetry before looks.

Verbal

  • Say the relation as a sentence; test each option; tighten if two fit.
  • Watch direction, part of speech, and “lack of” (craven : courage).
  • Similarities: the most general category true of both. Never a difference.
  • Key the standard meaning, not the clever one.

Numerical

  • Series: differences → 2nd differences → ratios → split odd/even → squares, cubes, primes, Fibonacci → alternating ops.
  • Rates add, times don't. Percents multiply. Equal-distance average speed is below the simple mean. n gaps need n + 1 posts.
  • Estimate first, plug the answer back in.

Logic

  • Syllogism: try to build a counterexample world. Two negatives = no conclusion.
  • P → Q gives not-Q → not-P only. “A only if B” = A → B. “A unless B” = not-B → A.
  • Seating: fixed facts, then pairs, then negatives; fix one seat at round tables; recheck every clue.

Working memory

  • Chunk 3-3-2. Rehearse in the same rhythm.
  • Backward: reverse inside chunks, say chunks last-first.
  • Sequencing: tick a mental number line; keep duplicates. Letter-number: numbers first, then letters.
  • Running span: rolling window of the last four.

Processing speed

  • Learn the key in row one. Steady pace; no erasing; never skip.
  • Symbol search: distinctive part first; orientation decides; one scan per target.

Test day

  • Budget = time ÷ items (Wonderlic about 14 s; Mensa Norway about 43 s, harder later). Checkpoints at ¼, ½, ¾.
  • No penalty on most tests: never leave a blank. At 2× budget with no rule, guess, flag, move.
  • One-to-one tests: you can't skip; always give a reasoned answer.
  • Sleep a normal night; no cramming. Caffeine: usual dose, usual time.
  • Nerves = readiness. One slow breath after a hard item; each item is one point.
  • Eat, glasses, arrive early. Practice removes avoidable losses; it doesn't change g.
